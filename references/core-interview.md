# Core Interview

Use this reference to design an adaptive eight-round conversation. The examples show the quality and shape of useful questions; they are not a script.

## Question design rules

Each question should:

- describe a decision, scene, reaction, or trade-off;
- be answerable without branding knowledge;
- contain 4–6 distinct options grounded in the founder's context;
- permit multiple choices only when the combination itself is useful;
- include a custom-answer path;
- reveal a decision the final foundation can use.

Avoid:

- “What is your mission?”
- “What are your values?”
- “Describe your target audience.”
- “Choose three adjectives for your brand.”
- options distinguished only by fashionable wording;
- asking the founder to solve the strategy instead of supplying evidence.

Exception: when the initial request contains no meaningful company context, the first Round 1 question is a short free-text grounding prompt defined in `SKILL.md`. Apply the option rules from the second question onward.

Write options in the founder's vocabulary where possible. Include one uncomfortable but plausible option when it may reveal a real trade-off. Never make the desired answer obvious.

## Round 1 — The company beneath the description

### Purpose

Understand the emotional reason the company needs to exist and the reputation the founder wants to earn.

### Question shapes

1. **Future recommendation:** “It is five years from now and someone recommends this company to a friend. Which sentence would make you think, ‘Yes—that is what I built’?”
2. **Reason for change:** Use a concrete contrast between what the founder is doing now and what they want more of.

### Option dimensions

- makes complexity understandable;
- produces unusually thoughtful work;
- creates disproportionate practical value;
- restores relevance to overlooked people or businesses;
- applies emerging ideas before they become obvious;
- repeatedly invents products of its own;
- builds a respected specialist practice;
- creates independence or a particular way of living.

### Listen for

Purpose, desired reputation, dissatisfaction, founder ambition, emotional stakes, and whether the company exists mainly for customers, craft, independence, change, or a combination.

## Round 2 — Work, energy, and the future shape

### Purpose

Separate the work that genuinely energizes the founder from work they merely know how to sell. Clarify the intended business shape without assuming growth.

### Question shapes

1. **Free Monday:** “With no client obligations and financial security for the day, what work would you start without being told?”
2. **Healthy future:** Present distinct futures such as a tiny profitable company, premium practice, product portfolio, community institution, scalable platform, or one breakout company.

### Adaptation

When the founder selects several scales, ask whether they represent stages, alternatives, or simultaneous ambitions. Convert the answer into horizons rather than forcing one permanent endpoint.

### Listen for

Founder-market fit, ownership preference, recurring versus project revenue, appetite for team growth, product versus service identity, and the private definition of success.

## Round 3 — People and problems worth caring about

### Purpose

Identify primary and secondary audiences through empathy and repeated interest, not demographic labels.

### Question shapes

1. **Person worth helping repeatedly:** Describe people in situations, not segments—for example, an overlooked business owner, an expert with no technical partner, a team buried in repetitive work, or a self-serve customer.
2. **Problem test:** Ask which problem characteristics deserve years of attention: pain, frequency, neglect, emotional meaning, economic value, visible transformation, or personal curiosity.

### Adaptation

If the founder names many audiences, distinguish:

- the desired future customer;
- the current paying customer;
- opportunity audiences worth exploring;
- people the company is not designed to serve.

### Listen for

User situation, purchasing relationship, accessibility needs, market breadth, problem intensity, and whether the audience is chosen from evidence, empathy, convenience, or aspiration.

## Round 4 — Presence and anti-identity

### Purpose

Reveal personality through social presence and define the perception the founder most wants to avoid.

### Question shapes

1. **Entering a room:** “Before anyone knows what the company does, what kind of presence should they feel?” Use options such as quiet expert, curious inventor, warm guide, sharp operator, bold challenger, or meticulous craftsperson.
2. **Most painful misreading:** Build options from the founder's context: generic agency, lifestyle hobby, faceless corporation, trend chaser, body shop, luxury without substance, worthy but dull, or founder personality cult.

### Adaptation

When two personality choices create a useful tension, preserve both and define the boundary:

- playful without childishness;
- expert without distance;
- bold without aggression;
- premium without exclusivity;
- warm without sentimentality.

### Listen for

Personality, voice, desired emotional temperature, anti-positioning, and cues the identity must avoid.

## Round 5 — Decisions when nobody is watching

### Purpose

Derive values from difficult choices and establish non-negotiable boundaries.

### Question shapes

1. **Hard trade-off:** Tailor a plausible decision involving speed versus polish, revenue versus focus, automation versus human care, reach versus depth, growth versus control, or convention versus experimentation.
2. **Profitable refusal:** “Even if it made money, what would feel wrong for this company to build or do?”

### Adaptation

Ask what behavior proves the selected principle. “Quality matters” is incomplete until the founder defines what may remain rough and what must never be careless.

### Listen for

Behavioral values, ethical posture, data and privacy expectations, quality threshold, independence, responsibility, and decision rules.

## Round 6 — The customer's emotional journey

### Purpose

Define the desired aftertaste of the experience and how a young or unfamiliar company earns trust.

### Question shapes

1. **First-use aftertaste:** Offer emotional outcomes such as relief, clarity, delight, confidence, momentum, belonging, control, or trust.
2. **Reason to believe:** Ask what should ultimately overcome hesitation: the product itself, transparency, founder credibility, responsive support, public expertise, community recommendation, track record, trialability, or institutional proof.

### Adaptation

If the founder chooses several emotions, ask which one is foundational and which are supporting. If trust depends on evidence the company does not yet have, distinguish present proof from future proof.

### Listen for

Customer-experience principles, trust signals, support posture, tone during failure, and the emotional sequence from discovery through continued use.

## Round 7 — Focus and meaningful difference

### Purpose

Clarify what the company chooses, what it competes against, and what advantage it intends to prove.

### Question shapes

1. **Idea or opportunity filter:** “Ten plausible opportunities appear. Which test should eliminate most of them?”
2. **Alternative choice:** “If this company did not exist, what would the customer do instead—and what is unsatisfying about that?”

### Option dimensions

- intensity or frequency of the problem;
- founder curiosity;
- healthy economics;
- speed to user value;
- small-team advantage;
- underserved audience;
- craft advantage;
- proprietary access or expertise;
- behavior change required;
- the option of doing nothing.

### Guardrail

Do not declare a USP proven because it sounds distinctive. Record it as an intended advantage until customer behavior or evidence validates it.

### Listen for

Positioning, category, alternatives, product thesis, focus, intended differentiation, evidence, and strategic risk.

## Round 8 — Story, identity, and practical constraints

### Purpose

Capture the meaning already attached to the company and the constraints a future collaborator must respect.

### Question shapes

Choose the two most relevant based on what remains uncovered:

- “What does the name mean or evoke for you personally?”
- “How visible should the founder be in the public identity over time?”
- When status is genuinely ambiguous: “Besides the elements you have already fixed, is anything else provisional or open?”
- “How should the company's place of origin be felt, if at all?”
- “If several products or offers emerge, how visibly should they belong to the same family?”
- “What social or cultural assumptions would misrepresent the company?”
- “What should a designer preserve as they extend the wider identity around the fixed elements?”

### Existing-material rule

Existing material is fixed by default. Document its role and the practical constraints it creates for the wider system; do not evaluate whether it is strategically or aesthetically good. Critique it only when the founder explicitly changes its status.

### Listen for

Origin story, name meaning, founder visibility, culture, geography, brand architecture, current identity, fixed assets, and practical handoff requirements.

## Minimum coverage checklist

After round eight, verify that the conversation supports a responsible answer to each area:

- why the company exists now;
- the future it is trying to create;
- founder definition of success;
- primary future audience;
- secondary or current audiences;
- problems or opportunities worth pursuing;
- business or operating model;
- intended difference and relevant alternatives;
- behavioral principles and boundaries;
- personality and voice;
- desired customer emotions;
- trust and proof;
- history and founder relationship;
- cultural or geographic influence;
- existing identity constraints;
- intentionally open decisions.

## Gap handling

Not every area requires another question.

Ask a gap question only when:

- the missing answer would change positioning or output materially;
- two plausible interpretations lead to different decisions;
- the founder requested a comprehensive foundation and the omission would make it unusable.

Mark a topic intentionally open when:

- evidence does not yet exist;
- the answer depends on the first product or market;
- preserving flexibility is a deliberate choice;
- forcing certainty would create fiction.

## Core checkpoint format

Keep this short:

```markdown
### What is already clear
- [Specific signal]
- [Specific signal]

### Productive tensions
- [Tension and current reconciliation]

### Still open
- [Material gap or deliberate uncertainty]

Would you like to synthesize now, deepen selected expression topics, or resolve only the remaining material gaps?
```
